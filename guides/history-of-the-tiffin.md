## Where the word comes from

"Tiffin" is an Anglo-Indian word from the British period for a light midday meal. It is usually traced to an old English dialect word for taking a small drink or sip. In India the meaning stuck and widened: tiffin can mean the meal, the lunchbox that carries it, or the break in which you eat it. Ask anyone who went to school in South Asia about the "tiffin break" and you will hear about the bell, the scramble, and trading parathas for noodles.

## The stacked carrier

The classic tiffin carrier is a set of two to four round stainless-steel tins that stack on top of each other, held together by a frame with a clasp and a handle on top. Each tier holds one part of the meal: dal in one, sabzi in another, rice in a third, rotis in the lid or a fourth. The design keeps wet and dry food apart, lets one person carry a full meal in one hand, and lasts for decades.

Modern versions add insulated outer jackets, silicone seals and smaller tiers for children, but the stack is unchanged.

## Mumbai's dabbawalas

In Mumbai, the tiffin became a delivery system. Dabbawalas collect lunches from homes in the morning, carry them by bicycle, handcart and suburban train to offices across the city, and return the empty tins in the afternoon. The Mumbai Dabbawala Association dates the service to roughly the 1890s, when a banker hired the first dabbawala, and credits Mahadeo Havaji Bachche with organising it into a team.

Commonly cited figures put the network at about 5,000 dabbawalas delivering around 200,000 lunches a day. The tins carry painted codes of letters, numbers and colours that tell each handler where the tin came from, which station it passes through and which building it goes to. The system's accuracy has made it a favourite case study in business schools.

The association today also runs talks, an experience day with a dabbawala, and a roti bank that redistributes surplus food.

## The tiffin goes global

Wherever South Asian families settled, tiffin services followed: home cooks in Brampton, Southall, Edison and Melbourne began selling monthly meal plans to students, workers and families who missed home food. Reporting in Toronto has linked the recent growth in demand to the return to offices, rising grocery bills and appetite for regional cooking that restaurants rarely serve. The steel carrier itself has gone further still, sold worldwide as a plastic-free lunchbox.
