## Start with how you will actually eat

Before you compare kitchens, decide what you need the tiffin to do.

- **Lunch, dinner or both?** Lunch-only plans are cheaper but you still cook every evening. Lunch-and-dinner plans usually cost close to double.
- **How many days?** Five-day plans suit office workers. Students and shift workers often need six or seven.
- **Where will you eat it?** If there is a microwave at work, chilled delivery and sealed containers are fine. If not, you want a kitchen that delivers hot food in insulated tins close to lunchtime.
- **What must never be in it?** Jain, no onion-garlic, halal, vegan, nut allergies, low oil. Say it at the start, not after the first delivery.

## Compare the tiffin, not the monthly price

Two plans at the same price can be very different meals. Ask each kitchen for a written description of one standard tiffin and count it.

| What to count | What to ask |
|---|---|
| Rotis or parathas | How many, and is the number fixed? Are they made with oil or ghee? |
| Rice | Roughly how much? Is it plain or a pulao on some days? |
| Sabzi and curry | How many tins, and how big? Is there a dry sabzi and a gravy, or one of each on alternate days? |
| Dal | Every day, or rotated with kadhi or sambar? |
| Extras | Salad, raita, pickle, papad, sweet: which days? |

Then work out the price per tiffin: divide the monthly price by the number of delivery days. A CA$200 plan with 22 deliveries is about CA$9 a tiffin. A CA$180 plan with 20 deliveries is CA$9 too, but one fewer week of food than you might assume.

## Ask to see next week's rotation

Good kitchens run a weekly or two-weekly rotation and will show it to you. A rotation tells you three things: whether the menu is varied, whether the kitchen plans ahead, and whether there is a dish you dislike on two days of every week. If the answer is "whatever we make that day", expect inconsistency.

## Check registration and hygiene

Every country has a rule for people who cook food to sell. Ask for the registration or permit number, not a promise.

- **Canada:** home kitchens notify their local public health unit (Ontario) or hold a provincial permit (Québec's MAPAQ, Alberta Health Services). Ask which.
- **United Kingdom:** food businesses register with the local council and get a food hygiene rating you can look up on the Food Standards Agency website.
- **India:** look for the 14-digit FSSAI registration or licence number. It must appear on packaging.
- **California:** permitted home kitchens show their county permit number and the words "Made in a Home Kitchen" in their advertising.

A kitchen that is honest about its status, even if registration is in progress, is a better bet than one that changes the subject.

## Get the delivery details in writing

Delivery problems cause more cancellations than food. Ask:

- What is the delivery window, and how late is too late?
- Do they deliver to your door, your floor, your reception desk or only the street?
- Are the tins returnable steel with a deposit, or disposable containers?
- What happens on public holidays and in bad weather?

## Know the pause, skip and cancel rule

Ask how much notice you need to pause for a day, a week or a holiday, whether paused days carry over or are lost, and how refunds work if you stop mid-month. A day's notice to pause is common and reasonable. Losing paid days because you were travelling is not.

## Always start with a trial

Almost every kitchen offers a paid trial meal or a trial week. Use it to judge the food at lunchtime, not straight from the stove: is the rice still soft, is the sabzi still good after four hours, did it arrive on time? Then decide.

## Red flags

- No written menu or price list.
- Will not say where the food is cooked.
- "Unlimited" portions with no definition of a standard tiffin.
- Prices that change without notice, or cash only with no receipt.
- A long minimum commitment before you have tried a single meal.
