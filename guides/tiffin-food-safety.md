## The danger zone and the two-hour rule

Bacteria multiply fastest between 4°C and 60°C (40°F and 140°F). US food-safety guidance says perishable food should not sit at room temperature for more than **2 hours**, or more than **1 hour** when it is above 32°C (90°F).

A typical tiffin is packed at 8 am and eaten at 1 pm: five hours. So unless something keeps it hot or cold, a tiffin with rice, dal, paneer, curd, egg or meat is outside the rule by lunchtime. That is why the way you pack matters more than what you pack.

## Packing a hot tiffin

1. Fill the insulated tin or jar with boiling water and leave it for a few minutes.
2. Empty it.
3. Add the food while it is steaming, at 60°C (140°F) or hotter.
4. Close the lid and keep it closed until lunch.

Preheating makes a large difference: a cold steel tin pulls heat out of the food in the first few minutes. Hot packing suits dal, khichdi, rajma, pulao and keema.

## Packing a cold tiffin

Use an insulated bag with **at least two cold sources**: two ice packs, or one ice pack and a frozen water bottle or juice box. Keep perishable food in the fridge until you leave home. Cold packing suits curd rice, salads, wraps with chutney, and anything with curd or paneer.

If there is a fridge at work or school, use it. Then the clock effectively stops until lunch.

## Foods that need extra care

- **Rice and cooked grains.** Rice can carry bacterial spores that survive cooking, and the toxins some bacteria produce are not destroyed by reheating. Never leave cooked rice warm for long: pack it piping hot in a preheated tin, or cool it quickly (spread it out on a tray) and keep it cold.
- **Curd, paneer and milk.** These sour and spoil quickly in the heat. Pack them in the cold tier, separately from hot food.
- **Eggs.** Cook them all the way through for a tiffin. No soft yolks, no wet scrambled eggs.
- **Chicken and mince.** Cook to 74°C (165°F) in the thickest part, then keep hot or cold, never lukewarm.
- **Sprouts.** Raw sprouts carry a higher food-safety risk. Steam them for a few minutes, especially for children, older adults and anyone pregnant.
- **Cut fruit and dressed salads.** Keep cold, and dress salads at lunch.

Dry, fully cooked foods such as thepla, khakhra and roasted snacks are much lower risk, which is why they have always been the tiffin of long journeys.

## Pack by tier

A stacked tiffin is ideal for separating hot and cold: hot dal and rice in an insulated tier, a cold tier with curd or salad and an ice pack in the bag beside it, and dry rotis or theplas wrapped on their own. Keep wet and dry foods apart so breads stay soft and crisp foods stay crisp.

## Cleaning steel tins

Wash every tier, lid and gasket in hot soapy water after each use, and pull silicone seals out of their grooves now and then: that is where food hides. Dry everything completely before stacking. Throw away leftovers at the end of lunch rather than bringing them home to eat later.

## When in doubt

If a tiffin smells off, looks bubbly, or simply sat somewhere warm for too long, throw it out. Babies, pregnant people, older adults and anyone with a weakened immune system should follow stricter advice from their doctor or local health authority.
